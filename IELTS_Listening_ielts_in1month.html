Part 4 · Questions 31–40

Speaker When we look at theories of education and learning we see a constant shifting of views as established theories are questioned and refined or even replaced, and we can see this very clearly in the way that attitudes towards bilingualism have changed.

Speaker Let’s start with a definition of bilingualism, and for our purposes today, we can say it’s the ability to communicate with the same degree of proficiency in at least two languages.

Speaker Now, in practical terms this might seem like a good thing – something we’d all like to be able to do.

Speaker However, early research done with children in the USA in fact suggested that being bilingual interfered in some way with learning and with the development of their mental processes, and so in those days bilingualism was regarded as something to be avoided, and parents were encouraged to bring their children up as monolingual – just speaking one language.

Speaker But this research, which took place in the early part of the twentieth century, is now regarded as unsound for various reasons, mainly because it didn’t take into account other factors such as the children’s social and economic backgrounds.

Speaker Now, in our last lecture we were looking at some of the research that’s been done into the way children learn, into their cognitive development, and in fact we believe now that the relationship between bilingualism and cognitive development is actually a positive one – it turns out that cognitive skills such as problem solving, which don’t seem at first glance to have anything to do with how many languages you speak, are better among bilingual children than monolingual ones.

Speaker And quite recently there’s been some very interesting work done by Ellen Bialystok at York University in Canada, she’s done various studies on the effects of bilingualism and her findings provide some evidence that they might apply to adults as well, they’re not just restricted to children.

Speaker So how do you go about investigating something like this?

Speaker Well, Dr Bialystok used groups of monolingual and bilingual subjects, aged from 30 right up to 88.

Speaker For one experiment, she used a computer program which displayed either a red or a blue square on the screen.

Speaker The coloured square could come up on either the left-hand or the right-hand side of the screen.

Speaker If the square was blue, the subject had to press the left ‘shift’ key on the keyboard and if the square was red they had to press the right shift key.

Speaker So they didn’t have to react at all to the actual position of the square on the screen, just to the colour they saw.

Speaker And she measured the subjects’ reaction times by recording how long it took them to press the shift key, and how often they got it right.

Speaker What she was particularly interested in was whether it took the subject longer to react when a square lit up on one side of the screen – say the left, and the subject had to press the shift key on the right-hand side.

Speaker She’d expected that it would take more processing time than if a square lit up on the left and the candidates had to press a left key.

Speaker This was because of a phenomenon known as the ‘Simon effect’, where, basically the brain gets a bit confused because of conflicting demands being made on it – in this case seeing something on the right, and having to react on the left – and this causes a person’s reaction times to slow down.

Speaker The results of the experiment showed that the bilingual subjects responded more quickly than the monolingual ones.

Speaker That was true both when the squares were on the ‘correct’ side of the screen, so to speak, and – even more so – when they were not.

Speaker So, bilingual people were better able to deal with the Simon effect than the monolingual ones.

Speaker So, what’s the explanation for this?

Speaker Well, the result of the experiment suggests that bilingual people are better at ignoring information which is irrelevant to the task in hand and just concentrating on what’s important.

Speaker One suggestion given by Dr Bialystok was that it might be because someone who speaks two languages can suppress the activity of parts of the brain when it isn’t needed – in particular, the part that processes whichever language isn’t being used at that particular time.

Speaker Well, she then went on to investigate that with a second experiment, but again the bilingual group performed better, and what was particularly interesting, and this is I think why the experiments have received so much publicity, is that in all cases, the performance gap between monolinguals and bilinguals actually increased with age – which suggests that bilingualism protects the mind against decline, in some way the life-long experience of managing two languages may prevent some of the negative effects of aging.

Speaker So that’s a very different story from the early research.

Speaker So what are the implications of this for education …
